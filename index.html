    Supporting India’s public health system since 2007

    William J. Clinton Foundation (WJCF) is a not‑for‑profit organisation registered under Section 8 of the Companies Act, 2013 (previously Section 25 of the Companies Act, 1956). Since its incorporation in 2007, WJCF has worked with central and state governments and other partners to improve health outcomes and strengthen health systems across India.

    We combine public health expertise, evidence, technology and implementation experience to identify critical gaps, develop practical solutions, and support approaches that can be integrated and sustained within the health system.

    Our role

    Our role is to help turn evidence into government‑led change. We combine strategic analysis, solution activation and stakeholder alignment to understand problems, design and implement solutions, and bring together the partners, capabilities and resources needed to achieve impact at scale.

    Our purpose

    Our vision

    A country in which everyone is able to live a healthy and fulfilling life.

    Our mission

    To save lives and improve health outcomes in India by enabling the government and private sector to strengthen and sustain quality health systems.

    India’s health needs continue to evolve. Some challenges require new solutions; others require proven interventions to work better, reach further or become part of routine health systems. WJCF works with government and other partners across this continuum, from identifying emerging needs and testing new approaches to strengthening and improving established programmes.

    From identifying a gap to lasting change

    We start with the problem, not a predetermined solution. Depending on the need, our role may involve generating evidence, introducing a technology, strengthening programme delivery, shaping markets, building capacity, improving data systems or bringing partners together around a shared public health priority.

    Across our work, we follow three broad pathways: Initiate, Institutionalise and Improve.

    01

    Initiate — Identify the need and build the solution

    We work with government and partners to understand health needs, identify barriers and opportunities, generate evidence, and design solutions that are practical, cost‑effective and suited to the context.

    02

    Institutionalise — Take what works into the system

    When an intervention demonstrates value, we support the government to institutionalise the most efficient and cost‑effective products, processes and programmes in the health system.

    03

    Improve — Use evidence and data to make systems work better

    We use monitoring, analytics, implementation research and emerging technologies to understand what is working, identify where course correction is needed, and continuously improve programme quality, efficiency and reach.

    We design for ownership and sustainability from the outset: integrating solutions into existing institutions, building local capacity, and working towards financing and implementation models that can continue beyond our direct support.

    • Infectious Diseases

      Tuberculosis, Malaria, Dengue, Lymphatic Filariasis and Kala‑azar, HIV, Syphilis and Hepatitis B (Triple Elimination)

    • Infectious Diseases

      Supporting stronger systems for prevention, early detection, treatment and disease elimination.

      The challenge

      India has made major gains against infectious diseases, but the remaining burden is increasingly concentrated and harder to reach. India continues to carry nearly a quarter of the global tuberculosis burden, with more than 27 lakh people affected. At the same time, malaria cases and deaths have fallen sharply over the past decade, shifting the challenge from broad control to identifying and responding to the remaining pockets of transmission.

      Across tuberculosis, malaria, lymphatic filariasis, visceral leishmaniasis, dengue, HIV, syphilis and hepatitis B, the next gains depend on finding infections earlier, improving last‑mile access to diagnostics and treatment, and keeping surveillance sensitive enough to detect residual transmission and outbreaks. Antimicrobial resistance adds a further challenge: when drugs and diagnostic options are limited, targeted treatment and evidence‑led public‑health action become more difficult.

      Our approach

      Find people earlier

      Strengthen community‑based and risk‑based screening, decentralised diagnostics and last‑mile service delivery so people who may be missed by symptom‑only or facility‑based pathways can be identified sooner.

      Strengthen the pathway from diagnosis to care

      Improve laboratory networks, referral, treatment, preventive therapy, commodity systems and follow‑up so that detection leads to timely and continuous care.

      Use data and technology responsibly

      Support surveillance, dashboards, review systems, predictive analytics and responsible use of AI‑enabled tools as decision support within government programmes, with human oversight and clear programme use cases.

      Build systems that can sustain gains

      Generate implementation evidence, strengthen programme management and workforce capacity, and support market‑shaping and institutionalisation where diagnostics, medicines or delivery models need to become more accessible at scale.

      Our impact

      • TUBERCULOSIS15.09 lakh+ people reached

        AI‑enabled active case finding across 11 states identified 11,593 people with TB. Nearly one‑third of microbiologically confirmed diagnoses were among people without symptoms.

      • LYMPHATIC FILARIASIS13 of 14 MDA states

        LF reporting was integrated into IHIP across 13 of 14 mass drug administration states, VBD drugs and diagnostics into DVDMS for all states, and the national LF guidelines were revised for the first time since 2009 with WJCF support.

      • TRIPLE ELIMINATION13.9 lakh HIV | 14.6 lakh syphilis | 16.4 lakh hepatitis B

        Screenings in West Bengal’s statewide triple elimination programme. In the pilot districts, timely hepatitis B immunoglobulin for exposed infants rose from 70% to 98%.

      • DENGUE5 zones | 369 wards

        Weekly risk predictions across five Bengaluru zones and ward‑level heatmaps covering 369 wards support more targeted dengue prevention and response planning.

      • AI CHEST X‑RAYS for TB DetectionUnder US$1 per scan

        An 83% price reduction for AI‑enabled digital chest X‑rays, with the supplier base growing from 3 to 10, has enabled 1,000+ planned procurements.

      • DUAL HIV–SYPHILIS TESTS~70 lakh kits procured

        Adoption across 10+ states brought syphilis testing to near price parity with HIV tests; syphilis screening rose about 1.5 times, reaching 85% coverage among pregnant women in FY 2023–24.

    • Vaccine‑Preventable Diseases

      Routine Immunisation, Vaccine‑Preventable Disease Surveillance

    • Vaccine‑Preventable Diseases

      Reaching missed children while strengthening the surveillance systems that protect population‑level gains.

      The challenge

      India’s Universal Immunisation Programme is one of the largest in the world, reaching around 2.67 crore infants and 2.9 crore pregnant women every year. The country has also sustained major public‑health gains, including polio‑free status since 2014 and elimination of maternal and neonatal tetanus since 2015.

      The next challenge is more targeted: finding zero‑dose and under‑immunised children, reducing dropouts, improving coverage in urban and mobile populations, and making routine programme data more actionable. At the same time, disease surveillance must remain highly sensitive even as incidence falls. The transition of vaccine‑preventable disease surveillance into government systems therefore requires both continuity of case detection and sustained institutional capacity.

      Our approach

      India’s vaccine‑preventable disease surveillance is being strengthened and transitioned through a dedicated Programme Management Unit working within the Central Surveillance Unit of IDSP‑NCDC, working under government leadership and alongside the teams that will carry surveillance forward.

      Strengthen management and data use

      Integrate review tools, data‑quality processes, triangulation and decision tracking into routine immunisation management so district and block teams can identify gaps and act on them.

      Design for zero‑dose and underserved families

      Use human‑centred design, behavioural science, GIS and community feedback to understand why children are missed and equip frontline workers with practical tools for more effective last‑mile engagement.

      Improve urban immunisation systems

      Support area demarcation, microplanning, supportive supervision, U‑WIN adoption and public‑private coordination so services can keep pace with dense, mobile and underserved urban populations.

      Support resilient VPD surveillance

      Under IDSP‑NCDC leadership, support the policy and operational framework, governance and financial planning, a tracked process for a smooth transition, state and district reviews through performance analytics and technical assistance, capacity building, and innovative solutions.

      Our impact

      • NATIONAL ADOPTION143 districts | 11 states

        The Ministry of Health and Family Welfare made the management and governance approach, first piloted with Uttar Pradesh and Bihar with WJCF support, a core component of the Universal Immunisation Programme.

      • STATE SCALE‑UP165 districts | 3 states

        Uttar Pradesh formally adopted the RI Review Tool in 65 of 75 districts, Madhya Pradesh scaled the model to all 52 districts, and Bihar is rolling it out across all 38.

      • DATA FOR DECISIONS1.2 lakh+ immunisation activities

        Review meetings, microplanning sessions and supervision visits were supported through digital tools, as data completeness rose by 45 percentage points, with accuracy above 95%.

      • ZERO‑DOSE LEARNING22,500+ caregivers reached

        Tools co‑created with families and frontline workers reached 22,500+ caregivers and equipped 1,320+ ASHAs and 120 ANMs. In urban Patna, GIS‑based enumeration found about three times as many zero‑dose children as earlier headcounts.

      • URBAN IMMUNISATION1.02 lakh infants registered on U‑WIN

        Across four focus cities, 100% of target infants were registered on U‑WIN, more than 90% of past vaccination records were updated, and 26 private delivery points were onboarded.

      • VPD SURVEILLANCE12,691 reporting units

        Across the phased transition to IDSP‑NCDC, 12,691 reporting units have sustained case detection and reporting, with programme management support from WJCF.

    • Women and Children’s Health

      Sexual and Reproductive Health, Maternal and Newborn Health

    • Women and Children’s Health

      Strengthening informed choice, quality care and continuity across the reproductive, maternal and newborn health journey.

      The challenge

      India has made substantial progress in family planning and reproductive health, including a contraceptive prevalence rate of 66.7% and achievement of replacement‑level fertility. Yet the quality and consistency of access still vary. NFHS‑5 data show that 64% of first births occur within the first three years of marriage and 57% of subsequent births occur less than two years after the previous birth, reinforcing the importance of informed choice, birth spacing and reliable access to a broad range of contraceptive options.

      Maternal and newborn survival has also improved, but preventable risk remains. India reduced its maternal mortality ratio from 93 deaths per 1,00,000 live births in 2019–21 to 87 in 2022–24. Neonatal mortality also declined to 18 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2025 but remains above the Sustainable Development Goal target of 12 with significant disparities across states. The continuing challenge is to translate coverage into consistent quality: identifying risk early, ensuring facility readiness, maintaining essential supplies, and linking women and newborns to timely care when complications arise. WJCF supports the governments of Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh to reduce maternal and neonatal mortality.

      Our approach

      Expand equitable access and informed choice

      Strengthen counselling, provider readiness, supply systems and delivery channels so women and young people can access reliable family planning and SRH information, products and services with privacy and informed choice.

      Strengthen maternal and newborn service readiness

      Support protocols, diagnostics, clinical capacity, referral pathways and essential commodities for high‑risk pregnancy, obstetric complications and newborn respiratory care within existing public systems.

      Design around people’s needs

      Use behavioural insights, human‑centred design and digital channels to understand where people drop out of care and make information and services easier to navigate without creating parallel delivery systems.

      Use evidence to improve programmes and markets

      Translate programme data and research into service improvements, supply planning and market‑shaping strategies that can expand method choice, improve continuity and inform government‑led scale.

      Our impact

      • FAMILY PLANNING SERVICE DELIVERY47 public facilities resumed services

        Stronger supply management and facility readiness enabled previously inactive facilities in three Madhya Pradesh districts to restart Antara, IUD and postpartum IUD services.

      • FACILITY READINESS730+ public facilities assessed

        More than 630 Health and Wellness Centres and 103 higher‑level facilities received improvement plans covering workforce, commodities, privacy, infection prevention and demand generation.

      • DIGITAL FP‑SRH ACCESS29,300 first‑time category purchasers

        During a four‑month campaign in partnership with an e‑pharmacy, 29,300 users with no FP‑SRH purchase in the previous 12 months made a purchase, linking digital engagement to uptake.

      • DIGITAL FP‑SRH UPTAKE7,019 → 11,354 monthly purchasers

        Average monthly FP‑SRH purchasers rose from 7,019 before the pilot to 11,354 during the campaign, using category‑level, brand‑agnostic content focused on informed choice.

      • POSTPARTUM FAMILY PLANNING17.5% vs 9% uptake

        Counselling focused on the few drivers identified raised postpartum contraceptive uptake among first‑time mothers in Madhya Pradesh from a 3–6% baseline to 17.5%, against 9% in comparison areas, within five months.

      • MARKET SHAPING~50% lower prices

        Global market shaping helped cut contraceptive implant prices by about half, and demand forecasting supported a ~50% reduction in ferric carboxymaltose procurement prices across five states.

    • Health Systems Strengthening

      Digital Health, Care Coordination, Medical Oxygen, Integrated Lung Health Screening (iLIFT), Health Analytics and AI Unit

    • Health Systems Strengthening

      Connecting integrated screening, digital health, data and AI, care coordination and resilient essential services to make quality care easier to access and sustain.

      The challenge

      India has made major investments in the building blocks of universal health coverage, including the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission, PM‑JAY, Ayushman Arogya Mandirs and expanded medical‑oxygen infrastructure. The next challenge is ensuring that these systems work seamlessly in everyday care.

      Digital adoption must extend across public and private providers, health information must remain useful across a patient’s journey, and people need clearer pathways to the right service and provider. Medical oxygen systems, meanwhile, require more than installed equipment: reliable maintenance, trained staff, clearly defined clinical protocols, robust data systems and predictable financing are essential for them to function as dependable components of routine healthcare delivery. Screening, too, is often fragmented: TB, chronic respiratory diseases and other non‑communicable diseases are largely screened through separate pathways, even though India has an estimated 5.5 crore people living with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. And as AB PM‑JAY and ABDM build one of the country’s largest health data repositories, the opportunity lies in turning that data into trusted insights for decisions.

      Our approach

      Enable sustainable digital health adoption

      Support facilities, providers and state teams to adopt interoperable digital workflows, strengthen consent‑based health‑record use and build the local capacity needed for sustained ABDM adoption.

      Make care easier to navigate

      Strengthen patient navigation, appointment and referral pathways so people can identify available services and reach appropriate care with fewer delays and unnecessary visits.

      Use technology with clear clinical purpose

      Support responsible use of digital tools and AI‑enabled decision support where they can improve triage, care coordination or programme management, while keeping clinical judgement and public‑system workflows central.

      Build resilient oxygen systems

      Provide support in strengthening governance, asset management, supply planning, workforce capacity, routine data reporting and predictable financing to ensure medical oxygen is established as an essential service across all levels of care.

      Integrate screening across conditions

      Use existing TB active case‑finding platforms as an entry point for screening chronic respiratory diseases, other non‑communicable diseases and sickle cell disease, with diagnosis and follow‑up available at block level.

      Turn programme data into decisions

      Support the National Health Authority to establish a Health Analytics and AI Unit that turns PM‑JAY and ABDM data into trusted, analytics‑ready datasets and insights, with governance and privacy safeguards.

      Our impact

      • DIGITAL HEALTH33 lakh+ ABHA‑linked records

        Across five states, 5,076 supported facilities adopted an ABDM‑enabled solution and generated more than 33 lakh consent‑linked health records.

      • DIGITAL HEALTH42% sustained record‑linking

        In Bhopal, 42% of private facilities that generated an ABHA‑linked record continued linking records after direct pilot support ended, versus 21% under Microsites.

      • CARE COORDINATION1.36 lakh+ appointments booked

        Through the State Health Agency’s Niramayam helpline in Madhya Pradesh: 24,500+ during the pilot, and more than 1.12 lakh across nearly 500 public and PM‑JAY‑empanelled hospitals in FY 2025–26, with 99% of attended appointments fulfilled.

      • MEDICAL OXYGEN10,000+ health workers trained

        Across three states, 800+ district hospitals, sub‑district hospitals and CHCs were supported, oxygen modules were integrated into state training programmes, and ₹20 crore+ is now allocated annually in state PIPs.

      • INTEGRATED SCREENING11,470 people screened

        In Korba, Chhattisgarh, 167 camps on a TB screening platform provided 10,261 AI‑enabled chest X‑rays and 1,897 spirometry tests. Portable spirometers cost ₹0.7 lakh per device, against ₹2.3 lakh through conventional procurement.

    • Non‑Communicable Diseases

      Type 1 Diabetes, Cervical Cancer, Sickle Cell Disease, Assistive Technologies: Presbyopia

    • Non‑Communicable Diseases

      Bringing prevention, diagnosis and lifelong care closer to people through stronger public health systems.

      The challenge

      Non‑communicable diseases increasingly require the public health system to manage both prevention and long‑term care. India has an estimated 8.2 lakh people living with Type 1 Diabetes, including around 2.76 lakh children and adolescents. Cervical cancer remains one of the country’s most preventable cancers, yet it still causes close to 80,000 deaths a year; in Rajasthan, NFHS‑5 found that only 0.4% of eligible women had ever been screened.

      Sickle cell disease presents a different but equally persistent challenge. India carries one of the world’s highest burdens, with cases concentrated disproportionately among tribal populations. Across these conditions, the recurring system gaps are similar: specialist‑dependent care, uneven access to essential commodities, weak follow‑up and referral, and limited availability of quality services closer to home. Presbyopia, the age‑related decline in near vision that becomes common after 40, shows a similar gap: it is simple to correct with reading glasses, yet screening and affordable spectacles are rarely available in primary care.

      Our approach

      Decentralise quality services

      Integrate screening, diagnosis and standardised care into existing NCD clinics, district and sub‑district facilities, primary‑care platforms and outreach systems, including near‑vision screening at Ayushman Arogya Mandirs, so people can access care closer to where they live.

      Strengthen the full care cascade

      Build referral, follow‑up, counselling and peer‑support systems so people remain connected from first screening or diagnosis through ongoing treatment and complication management.

      Secure commodities and provider readiness

      Strengthen forecasting and supply systems for insulin, glucose‑monitoring supplies, hydroxyurea, screening commodities, reading glasses and medicines while building the confidence of clinicians, nurses, CHOs and frontline workers.

      Use data to target and sustain care

      Support dashboards, registers and patient‑level tracking so government teams can identify gaps, focus on high‑burden populations and monitor whether people diagnosed are receiving appropriate ongoing care.

      Our impact

      • TYPE 1 DIABETES97 clinics | 3,101 enrolled

        T1D services were integrated into government NCD clinics across four states. By March 2026, 97 clinics were operational and 3,101 people were enrolled in structured care.

      • TYPE 1 DIABETESFirst national framework

        As part of the Technical Advisory Group, WJCF supported the Guidance Document for Diabetes Mellitus in Children, released with RBSK 2.0 in April 2026.

      • CERVICAL CANCER550+ devices | ~45% lower price

        Global price agreements cut the cost of portable thermal ablation devices by around 45%, and 550+ devices were introduced to decentralise cervical pre‑cancer treatment.

      • CERVICAL CANCER2.3 lakh+ women screened

        Between 2019 and 2022, systems strengthening across six states enabled cervical cancer screening for more than 2.3 lakh women. Under a new grant, WJCF now supports Rajasthan to bring screening and pre‑cancer treatment to community and primary health centres.

      • PRESBYOPIA60+ CHOs trained

        Community Health Officers in Sehore, Madhya Pradesh, were trained to screen adults for presbyopia, counsel them on near‑vision correction and dispense appropriate reading glasses.

      • SICKLE CELL DISEASE9,000+ health workers trained | ~22,000 enrolled in care

        Across 20 tribal‑majority districts of Madhya Pradesh, WJCF supported training of more than 9,000 health workers, as around 22,000 people with sickle cell disease were enrolled in care.

    • Climate and Environmental Health

      Clean Cooling, Lead Exposure

    • Climate and Environmental Health

      Addressing health risks shaped by climate, energy use and environmental exposure through market and system solutions.

      The challenge

      India’s cooling demand is rising rapidly. More than 100 crore air conditioners could be installed by 2050, while fewer than 10% of households currently own one. Without a shift towards more efficient cooling, growing demand will increase pressure on electricity systems and emissions at the same time that access to cooling becomes increasingly important for health and thermal comfort.

      Lead exposure is a different environmental risk but similarly requires action before harm occurs. IHME estimates that more than 23.3 crore children in India have blood lead levels above the WHO reference value of 5 µg/dL, alongside a substantial burden of premature mortality. The challenge spans unsafe products containing lead, limited testing options and the need for stronger surveillance and market incentives.

      Our approach

      Make efficient cooling more affordable

      Develop and test rebate, financing and replacement models that reduce the upfront cost of high‑efficiency air conditioners while aligning consumer, utility and climate benefits.

      Strengthen markets for cleaner technologies

      Work across manufacturers, retailers, recyclers, financiers and public institutions to address technology, supply‑chain and financing constraints that can slow adoption of more efficient products.

      Reduce lead exposure at source

      Support market transition strategies that accelerate safer pigments and lead‑free paints and to improve end‑of‑life management of lead‑acid batteries.

      Improve the tools to detect exposure

      Support supplier landscaping, target product profiles and market‑shaping pathways for affordable point‑of‑care blood‑lead diagnostics, alongside stronger biomonitoring and public‑health systems.

      Our impact

      • EFFICIENT COOLING10,000‑customer scale proposal

        Learning from the utility‑led high‑efficiency AC rebate pilot enabled TPDDL to submit a regulatory proposal to extend the model to 10,000 customers over three years.

      • COOLING FINANCE~US$200 million financing designed

        With IFC, WJCF co‑developed a ~US$200 million financing intervention to replace inefficient ACs, and supported BEE on India’s first national AC replacement framework. Four manufacturers with ~40% market share were engaged.

      • LEAD‑FREE PAINTSSafer‑input market pathway

        Market‑shaping work is developing strategies to accelerate safer pigments and reduce reliance on leaded raw materials, addressing exposure upstream through the paint and battery market.

      • BLOOD‑LEAD DIAGNOSTICSPoint‑of‑care testing pathway

        Supplier mapping, target product profile refinement and market analysis are being used to lower development barriers for next‑generation blood‑lead testing.

    • Diagnostics and Surveillance

      Disease Surveillance and Early Warning, Diagnostic Infrastructure

    • Diagnostics and Surveillance

      Strengthening the information and testing systems needed to detect threats earlier and respond faster.

      The challenge

      The COVID‑19 pandemic demonstrated how quickly public‑health response can be constrained when surveillance data are delayed, fragmented or incomplete. It also left India with a much larger molecular‑testing footprint, creating an opportunity to use existing infrastructure more efficiently for other priority diseases.

      Stronger preparedness therefore requires two things at once: surveillance systems that can detect unusual patterns early and diagnostics that are affordable, adaptable and connected to programme action. That means better data integration, stronger private‑sector reporting, trained teams, responsive governance and diagnostic pathways that fit real‑world public‑health settings.

      Our approach

      Detect signals earlier

      Support threshold‑based early‑warning systems, dashboards and structured reviews that help surveillance teams identify unusual patterns and trigger timely field investigation.

      Improve completeness and timeliness of reporting

      Strengthen public- and private‑sector reporting through simpler workflows, provider engagement and capacity building so surveillance platforms receive more decision‑useful data.

      Build institutional capacity

      Support governance, workforce development, outbreak‑response tools and integration into existing platforms so stronger surveillance practices can continue within government systems.

      Use diagnostic infrastructure more effectively

      Map molecular‑testing capacity, define feasible public‑health use cases and support regulatory and market analysis for adaptable diagnostic platforms that can serve multiple disease priorities.

      Our impact

      • EARLY WARNING3.4 crore people covered

        EWARS operated across 20 Madhya Pradesh districts and generated 342 alerts. District surveillance units responded to all alerts, with field investigation confirming 26 outbreaks.

      • PRIVATE‑SECTOR SURVEILLANCE118 facilities onboarded to IHIP

        A focused engagement model improved routine reporting, including a 138% increase in laboratory‑form reporting and a 52% increase in reporting days per week.

      • NATIONAL SURVEILLANCE TOOLS5 dashboards scaled nationally

        Five thematic disease‑surveillance dashboards, first piloted in Madhya Pradesh, were subsequently scaled nationally on the Integrated Health Information Platform.

      • DIAGNOSTIC INFRASTRUCTURE~7,900 RT‑PCR platforms mapped

        A pan‑India mapping exercise assessed the installed RT‑PCR base and explored how molecular‑testing infrastructure could support diseases beyond COVID‑19, including tuberculosis.
